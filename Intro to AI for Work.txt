Imagine a student collecting information from several school documents,
such as event notices or project instructions. Each document may organise
details differently, so the old way would be to open each document one
by one, find items like dates, names, locations, and costs, and copy
everything into a table by hand. That takes time and makes copy-and-paste
mistakes more likely.

With AI, the same task could become a simple workflow:
• Upload suitable documents and explain the goal.
• Ask AI to extract specific details, such as dates, names, locations
  and costs.
• Have AI organise the details into a clear table.
• Check the table against the original documents for accuracy.

This can reduce repetitive copying and leave more time for understanding
the information. Use only documents that your school or family allows
you to upload, and never share private information without permission.

This is just one example of how AI can help. The objective is to use
AI to save time, increase productivity and protect privacy.

When you think about AI, think about tasks that normally require human
thinking, such as understanding speech, recognising a face or choosing
a fast route.

Artificial intelligence is technology that allows computers to perform
tasks that normally require human intelligence.

AI does not automatically replace every developer or guarantee perfect
results; humans still define goals, check quality, and use it responsibly.

AI is already part of everyday life, even though it may seem new because
of ChatGPT.

When it comes to Netflix recommendations, Netflix suggests movies and
shows you may enjoy, helping you avoid scrolling through thousands of choices.
These recommendations account for about 80% of Netflix viewing, demonstrating
how useful AI can be for finding content quickly.

Google Maps has arrival-time predictions. This estimates how long a trip will
take and updates that estimate as conditions change. This helps people plan
when to leave and share a more realistic arrival time.

Billions of people rely on these predictions every day for commutes,
appointments and travel. AI is not only a recent chatbot trend; it has
been quietly helping people for years.

Before modern AI, engineers often created expert systems by programming
explicit, step-by-step rules for each situation.

A refrigerator is an example of this. A thermostat checks the temperature,
turns the cooling on when it is too warm, and turns it off once the target
temperature is reached.

Engineers can write these rules precisely, such as if the temperature is
above 4°C, turn the cooling on.

The refrigerator works because temperature control is simple and predictable.
Engineers can describe the needed behavior with a small set of precise rules.

Let's connect this to Netflix recommendations.

What rules do you think engineers could write to decide which movie or show
each person will enjoy?

Engineers might write rules such as recommending more movies from genres,
actors, directors, or themes a person has enjoyed before.

For example: if someone watches several science-fiction movies, recommend
more science-fiction movies. That rule may help, but it cannot cover every
person's changing and subjective preferences. Someone may enjoy science
fiction one day, a documentary the next, or dislike a movie despite its
genre.

Simple rules can describe a few factors, but no fixed rule list can reliably
predict everyone's taste.

The same limitation appears in Google Maps arrival-time predictions. Engineers
could write rules for ordinary traffic, weather, accidents or road construction,
but countless combinations can occur. A fixed rule list cannot anticipate every
situation or interaction between them.

The key insight is that many important tasks are too complex for humans to
describe as step-by-step instructions even when human experts can perform
them well.

So how did people solve this?

Instead of programming every rule, machine learning lets computers learn
procedures from examples. During training, the system receives many examples
such as emails marked spam or legitimate. It studies those examples to find
patterns that help it perform the task.

For instance, with enough labeled emails, it may learn that certain phrases,
senders, or formatting often appear in spam. Humans provide the examples and
correct labels, while the system adjusts what it has learned from those examples.

Train the AI System to Perform a Task
Examples -> Machine Learning -> Trained AI System

Once the system has learned patterns from many examples, it can receive a
new case and compare it with those learned patterns to produce a result.

For example, a spam filter examines a new email and checks whether its contents
resemble patterns commonly found in spam.

This is machine learning: the science of enabling computers to learn from data
and use learned patterns on new cases.

Let's see how this works with Netflix recommendations and Google Maps
arrival-time predictions.

Using the Trained AI System in the Real World
New Case -> Training AI System -> Result

For Netflix recommendations, during training, the system studies many users'
viewing histories, ratings and completion rates. It learns patterns connecting
people's preferences with features such as genres, actors and themes.

During operation, it compares your viewing profile with those learned patterns
and ranks titles you may enjoy. The system is not following one simple rule;
it is recognising many patterns learned from examples.

Now consider Google Maps arrival-time predictions. During training, the system
studies billions of real trips and learns patterns involving travel times, traffic,
weather, accidents and road conditions.

During operation, it compares the current conditions along your route with those
learned patterns and predicts your arrival time. Because conditions change, the
prediction can update as new information appears.

Machine learning works by recognising patterns learned during training and applying
them to new cases. Netflix uses viewing patterns to recommend content, while Google
Maps uses trip and traffic patterns to estimate arrival times.

The broader takeaway is that a major part of modern AI involves recognising patterns
in data and using those patterns to make predictions or decisions. This ability made
it possible to solve tasks that were too complex for traditional rule-based programming.

2. A hospital trains an AI system on thousands of medical scans labeled as normal
   or showing cancer. During training, what is the system learning?

a. The complete details of every scan in the training data

b. Patterns in medical scans that indicate cancer likelihood

c. A programmed checklist of cancer indicators that radiologists identified

d. How to copy the decisions that radiologists made for previous scans

Correct answers:
1. c. The patterns that indicate cancer are too complex and subtle for
      humans to write as explicit rules, even though radiologists can
      recognise them
2. b. Patterns in medical scans that indicate cancer likelihood

Complex medical patterns are difficult to express as fixed rules, and machine
learning learns patterns from labeled examples.

Imagine trying to write rules for a lung scan:
• If a spot is round, then call it cancer.
• If it's larger than a certain size, call it cancer.
• If it's near a certain area, then call it cancer.

These rules would fail because a harmless spot can be round or large, while
cancer can have many different shapes and sizes. This is what it means for
something to be difficult to express as fixed rules.

This is a concrete example of why some tasks cannot be handled well with a
fixed checklist.
